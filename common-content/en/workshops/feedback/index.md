Feedback is the foundation of effective collaboration. Both giving and receiving feedback are important communication skills to master.

We will practise giving and receiving feedback today. We will focus on one framework, SBI, and practise it many times. Then we will look at a second framework, GROW, for a different kind of conversation.

## Set up 🌼

- Make groups of 3. If you cannot, work in pairs (groups of 2).

## The Activity 🏆

Read the following scenarios and role-play each one.

- In your group, take turns giving and receiving feedback. One person gives feedback, one receives it, and one observes and gives comments. If you work in pairs (groups of 2), take turns giving and receiving feedback, then discuss and give comments on each other's feedback.

- After each scenario, switch roles so everyone gets a chance to try each role.

For the person giving feedback, remember to:

- Be direct
- Use clear language
- Talk about behavior, not personality
- Be kind

For the person receiving feedback, remember to:

- Be available and approachable
- Be accountable
- Steer clear of defensiveness
- Take a moment to reflect before responding
- Assume positive intent

### Practice Giving Feedback [35 mins]

- Spend **10 mins** reading about the SBI framework and talking about the example with your group.
- Then spend **5 mins** on each of the five scenarios, using the SBI framework. After each scenario, switch roles.

For each scenario:

1. **2 mins**: The giver gives feedback using SBI. The receiver responds.
2. **2 mins**: The observer uses the checklist below to give feedback on the feedback. Say one thing that went well and one thing to improve.
3. **1 min**: The giver tries again with the improved version.

#### 1. Situation, Behavior, Impact (SBI) framework

The [SBI Framework](https://www.revolutionlearning.co.uk/article/the-sbi-feedback-model/) structures feedback in the following order:

- **Situation**: Describe the situation. Say when and where it happened. This should be objective - no personal interpretations or feelings.
- **Behavior**: Describe the behavior observed. This should also be objective. Describe what you saw or heard, not what you think the person meant.
- **Impact**: Explain the effect of the behavior on you, your team, or the organization. This can be subjective - it's ok to talk about how things made you feel. But don't guess _why_ someone else did something.

#### Example: weak and strong feedback

**Scenario**: A team member often arrives late to meetings.

**Weak feedback**:

> You are always late. It is disrespectful. You clearly don't care about the team's time.

**Strong feedback**:

> In yesterday's stand-up and Monday's planning meeting (Situation), you joined about ten minutes after we started (Behavior). We had to repeat the points we had already covered, and the planning meeting ran over time. This delayed the next meeting for several people (Impact).

Discuss with your group:

- Why is the second version more likely to lead to a useful conversation?
- Find the Situation, Behavior and Impact in the strong example.
- What is wrong with the weak example?

#### Checklist for the observer

- **Situation**: Did they name a specific time and place? Did they avoid words like "always" and "never"?
- **Behavior**: Did they describe something you can see or hear? Did they avoid opinions about the person's personality or intentions?
- **Impact**: Did they explain the effect on themselves, the team or the project? Did they avoid guessing why the person did it?
- **Tone**: Was the feedback direct and kind?
- **Response**: Did they give the other person a chance to reply?

#### SBI scenarios

**Scenario 1: Missed deadlines**: A teammate often misses deadlines. When they finally deliver, the work is often not good enough, so the rest of the team must redo parts of it.

**Scenario 2: Positive feedback**: A colleague handled a difficult question from a client very well in a meeting. They stayed calm, said what they did not know, and sent a full answer later the same day. Give them feedback so they know exactly what to keep doing. (Positive feedback also needs a Situation, a Behavior and an Impact. "Great job!" does not tell anyone what to repeat.)

**Scenario 3: The long presentation**: A team member gives a useful presentation, but it is too long. It lasted more than an hour and the audience lost focus. The team will use the same presentation at the next client meeting. That meeting is only 1 hour long and needs to cover many other topics.

**Scenario 4: Interrupting others**: A colleague often interrupts others in team discussions. They also reject other people's ideas without thinking about them. The team finds this frustrating. (Be careful: "rude" is an opinion. What did you actually see and hear?)

**Scenario 5: A defensive response**: A teammate merged code without asking for a review. It broke the build for the whole team for an afternoon. When you talk to them, they answer defensively: "I only wanted to work faster. Nobody else was doing anything." The receiver plays the defensive person. The giver should stay with Situation, Behavior and Impact and not start an argument. The observer should notice when the conversation moves away from the feedback.

### Try a Different Approach: GROW [15 mins]

SBI works well when you want to tell someone what you saw. But sometimes a person already asks you for help, or has a goal they are working towards. In that case, a coaching approach can work better.

#### 2. Goal, Reality, Options, Will (GROW) framework

The [GROW framework](https://www.mindtools.com/an0fzpz/the-grow-model-of-coaching-and-mentoring) focuses on mentoring and guidance to give feedback.

- **Goal**: Explore the individual's goals
- **Reality**: Assess the current reality
- **Options**: Brainstorm options
- **Will**: Agree on a list of actions they will commit to, to reach the goal

**Scenario**: A colleague wants to be promoted to senior software engineer and has asked you for feedback on their performance. They have great technical skills. But you have noticed that they do not speak in meetings unless someone asks them directly, and they often wait to be given tasks instead of taking initiative.

Spend **5 minutes** on this scenario in your groups, using the same roles as before.

### Choose a Framework for Giving Feedback

We have looked at two frameworks for giving feedback:

- Situation, Behavior, Impact (SBI) - Describe the situation, detail the specific behavior you observed, and explain the impact it had.
- Goal, Reality, Options, Will (GROW) - Explore the individual's goals, assess the current reality, brainstorm options, and commit to action. This model is best when the person already knows the area they want to improve.

Spend **5 minutes** working through the following scenarios. Discuss with your partner which framework you would use, and why.

1. **Positive Feedback for Improvement** - A colleague made big improvements in their work, but there is still room for further development.

2. **Performance Review** - You are giving a formal performance review to a team member whose work is good, but who lacks creativity or initiative.

3. **Cross-Team Collaboration** - A team member from another department does not communicate well with your team. This causes delays and confusion.

Come together as a group and spend **5 minutes** discussing which framework you chose for each scenario and why. Your answers are unlikely to all be the same!

## Reflection 🧘‍♂️ [10 mins]

Come together as a group and discuss the following:

When giving feedback:

- What challenges did you face when giving feedback?
- Was it hard to keep Situation, Behavior and Impact separate? Did you add an opinion to the Behavior part? What did it sound like?
- How did you decide between SBI and GROW? Would you choose the same one in real life?
- Which framework do you find the most useful? Which one was harder for you?
- How did you balance critique with being supportive?
- Did the scenarios feel realistic or exaggerated? How would you change your approach in a real-world situation?
- How can feedback methods differ depending on the person or context?

When receiving feedback:

- How did the feedback make you feel? Was it easy to stay open to feedback?
- Were there any moments when you felt defensive? How did you manage those feelings? How might you manage them in real-world situations?
- Was the feedback clear and actionable? How would you use it to improve?
- How can you ask for more feedback from your peers or mentors?

## Wrap-up

In this session we practised giving feedback in different scenarios. We focused on SBI, and we looked at GROW as a second approach for a different kind of conversation.

SBI is a simple structure you can use in many situations. The more you practise it, the more natural it feels. When you give feedback in the future, try SBI first, and think about whether GROW would fit the situation better. After you give feedback, think about what went well and what did not. How well did you follow the framework? Did it fit the situation as well as you hoped? Use your answers to give even better feedback next time.

### Tips for Giving Feedback:

- Give feedback in the right context. Be careful with your tone and how it affects the other person.
- Give feedback soon after the event. Be direct and non-accusatory (assume positive intent).
- Give feedback privately.
- Sometimes it is better to ask first if someone would like feedback.

### Tips for Receiving Feedback:

- Receiving feedback is important for measuring your progress and understanding unspoken expectations.
- Ask for feedback early and often. [Asking for feedback late on a project or presentation can make us less open to criticism](https://hbr.org/2023/06/the-right-time-to-ask-for-feedback#:~:text=The%20best%20approach%20is%20to,annual%20performance%20review%20rolls%20around).
- Not all feedback will be positive. It is important to learn to accept both good and bad feedback calmly.
